In a busy market in northern Nigeria at midday, a vendor watches her tomatoes wilt under the burning sun. Here, the heat can spoil vegetables in just a few days.

In a shaded schoolyard in northern Nigeria that afternoon, Mohammed Bah Abba, a teacher in a white kaftan and cap, sets one clay pot inside a bigger one. He fills the gap with wet sand and lays a damp cloth over the top.

[video] In the same schoolyard as evening falls, soft vapour rises from the outer pot as the water evaporates. The heat drains away and the inner pot stays cool. Tomatoes that once rotted in days now stay fresh for weeks.

In a dark tin roofed home in Brazil on a bright morning, Alfredo Moser, a fifty year old man with grey hair, in a mechanic's blue overalls, fills a plastic bottle with water and a splash of bleach. Blackouts are common here, and the rooms stay gloomy even by day.

[video] In the same Brazilian home that morning, Alfredo Moser, a fifty year old man with grey hair, in a mechanic's blue overalls, pushes the bottle through a hole in the roof. Sunlight hits the water and the whole room lights up, almost as bright as a light bulb.

Over crowded rooftops in the Philippines at noon, thousands of bottles glint in the sun. A movement called Liter of Light carried his idea to hundreds of thousands of homes.

On the dry coastal hills of Chile's Atacama Desert at dawn, thick sea fog rolls in from the Pacific. This is one of the driest places on Earth, yet the air itself is full of water.

On the same Atacama hillside that morning, villagers in wool ponchos stretch a huge mesh net between two tall poles. Tiny droplets of fog cling to the mesh and slowly grow fatter.

At the foot of the Atacama net later that morning, water trickles into a gutter and fills a tank. Some nets collect hundreds of litres a day, enough to grow vegetables in the desert.

In a frozen Himalayan valley in Ladakh, India, on a winter afternoon, farmers stare at a dry irrigation channel. Spring is when their crops need water most, and that is exactly when it runs short.

[video] In the same Ladakh valley on a freezing winter night, Sonam Wangchuk, an engineer in a brown woollen jacket and cap, opens a pipe. Water shoots upward into the icy air and freezes into a towering cone of ice. He calls it an ice stupa.

In the Ladakh valley on a sunny spring morning, the ice stupa melts slowly. Its cone shape keeps most of it out of the sun, so water reaches the barley fields just when they need it.

In a misty rainforest in Meghalaya, India, early in the morning, Khasi villagers guide the roots of a rubber fig tree through a hollow betel nut trunk across a river. They are building a bridge that grows.

[video] In the same Meghalaya rainforest years later, on a golden afternoon, thick tangled roots span the river and a line of villagers walks across a double decker bridge. Unlike wood or steel, this bridge grows stronger as it ages.
